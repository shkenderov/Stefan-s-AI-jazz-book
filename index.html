Stefan's AABA Fake Book

Built from your YouTube Music playlists (uptempo swing, Lazy lindy, Bal/charleston, boogie-ish, it's complicated) and your band's own charts: a swing-dance book for the piano chair of a small band. Twenty-eight tunes are 32-bar A A B A; the other four (All the Things You Are, Autumn Leaves, All of Me, Just Friends) are the cousins you asked for, with their real forms marked. Charts are written the way the band reads them: barlines, boxed sections, repeats with first and second endings, and a simile mark when a bar repeats. Keys are the ones your band plays. The book is in seven parts, one per chair, each with all thirty-two tunes in the same alphabetical order: piano (concert, with the full notes and keyboard figures), trumpet (B♭), alto sax (E♭), tenor sax (B♭), guitar (with chord grids), bass and drums. The front matter is in concert pitch.

Bandstand basics for the piano chair

  1. With a bassist, give up the roots. Left hand lives around middle C on rootless voicings (3‑5‑7‑9 or 7‑9‑3‑5). At Charleston tempo, just 3rds and 7ths.
  2. Head in two, solos in four. Half-note feel on the melody, walking feel once the solos start. That's the Basie lift, and dancers feel it.
  3. Mark the bridge. Change texture at every B (thinner, or a different rhythm). Horns and dancers both read the form off you.
  4. Intro = the last four bars of the tune (or a I–VI–II–V vamp) at the real tempo. Ending = tag the last two bars three times.
  5. Guide tones first. Before you solo on a tune, play one chorus of nothing but 3rd+7th of every chord in the left hand. It's the shortest road to hearing a form.
  6. Play fewer chords at fast tempos. The bracketed turnarounds are decoration; the dancers hear the bass line and the drummer.

Pentatonics at dance tempo

At 200 bpm nobody can think a new scale every two beats, and nobody should. A pentatonic is five notes that sit under one hand, has no avoid notes, and covers eight bars at a time. Swing-era soloists (Lester Young above all) played the major pentatonic of the key through whole A sections and let the rhythm section supply the changes. The work is choosing the right five notes per section; after that the job is rhythm, not notes. Add the ♭5 to a minor pentatonic and you have the blues scale.

One pentatonic per section, tune by tune

  • I Got Rhythm · A: B♭ major pent (+ blue D♭). Bridge: the major pent of each dominant, two bars each (D, G, C, F), or stay on B♭ minor pent for the blues version.
  • Honeysuckle Rose · A: F major pent; drop to F minor pent in bars 5–8 for the blues. Bridge: F major pent over F7 and B♭, G major pent over G7.
  • Stompin' at the Savoy · A: D♭ major pent + E. Bridge: the major pent of each dominant (G♭, B, E, A, A♭).
  • Take the "A" Train · C major pent everywhere except bars 3–4, where no pentatonic fits the D7♭5: play its four notes (D F♯ A♭ C) plus E.
  • Sunny Side of the Street · C major pent plus the three money notes (G♯, C♯, F♯).
  • Blue Skies · A bars 1–5: C minor pent; bars 6–8: E♭ major pent. Bridge: E♭ major pent on the E♭ bars, E♭ minor pent on the A♭–6 bars.
  • Softly (in B♭–) · A: B♭ minor pent (the MJQ way). Bridge: D♭ major pent, then E♭ minor pent, then B♭ minor pent for the last two bars.
  • Bei Mir · C minor pent / blues scale, all 32 bars. F minor pent on the bridge's F– bars if you want contrast.
  • Moanin' · F minor pent / blues scale, all 32 bars. B♭ minor pent on the bridge's B♭–7.
  • Puttin' On the Ritz · A: F minor pent. Bridge: A♭ major pent (the sunlight), back to F minor pent on D♭7–C7.
  • All the Things You Are · A♭ major pent (bars 1–5), C major pent (6–8), E♭ major pent (9–13), G major pent (14–20), E major pent (21–23), A♭ major pent home. Five pentatonics, one tune.
  • Undecided · C major pent + blues; F7 bars take E♭. Bridge: C7 → F → D7 → G7, same as Sunny Side.
  • It Don't Mean a Thing · A bars 1–4: G minor pent; bars 5–8: B♭ major pent. Bridge: B♭ major pent, E♭ major on bars 5–6.
  • Jumpin' at the Woodside · B♭ major pent, all day; E♭ major pent on bars 5–6 if you want to move.
  • Lady Be Good · G major pent + blues; bridge: C major pent for two, G for two, then the three sharps (G♯ C♯ F♯).
  • Don't Get Around Much · C major pent with A7/D7/G7 money notes; bridge: F major pent then C7 → D7.
  • Blue Moon · E♭ major pent over the whole loop; bridge bars 5–6: G♭ major pent (= E♭ minor pent).
  • Ain't Misbehavin' · E♭ major pent; bridge: C minor pent for two bars, then F7 → C7 → F7 → B♭7 by their 3rds.
  • Lullaby of Birdland · F harmonic minor for bars 1–2, A♭ major pent for the rest, bridge included.
  • Satin Doll · one major pentatonic per ii–V: C, D, G, G♭, then C. Bridge: F major pent, G major pent.
  • Perdido · B♭ major pent + the B natural on G7. Bridge: same four dominants as I Got Rhythm.
  • Battle of Jericho · F minor pent / F blues, all 32 bars; E natural on the C7s if you want them heard.
  • Ridin' and Jivin' · A: F minor pent / blues over the loop. Bridge: A♭ major pent (E♭7, A♭), F major pent on the F7 bars.
  • Lester's Bounce · A: G minor pent / blues; the bass walk is the line. Bridge: G7 → C minor pent → A major pent → D7.
  • Blue Drag · D minor pent / blues; G minor pent on the bridge's G– bars.
  • A Smooth One · F major pent + blues over the riff. Bridge: B♭ major pent.
  • Autumn Leaves (in C–) · E♭ major pent for the major halves, C minor pent for the minor halves, A♭ major pent for the backdoor chain in C.
  • All of Me (in F) · F major pent; D minor pent on A7 → D–; B♭ major then B♭ minor pent on the last eight.
  • Just Friends (in G) · G major pent; B♭ major pent on C–7 F7, A♭ major pent on B♭–7 E♭7, A major pent on the A7.
  • Comes Love (in A–) · A minor pent; A harmonic minor on the E7s; D minor pent on the bridge.
  • Kansas City Stride (in G–) · G minor pent / blues on the riff; bridge: C major pent for six bars (G7, C, A–7), B♭ minor pent for the B♭– bars.
  • Topsy (in A–) · A minor pent / blues on the riff bars, D minor pent on the second A's D– bars; bridge: the major pent of each dominant (A, D, G, C), two bars each.

Piano angle: stack a pentatonic in fourths and you get the "So What" voicing. D G C E A under one hand is C major pentatonic (and A minor pentatonic) in one shape; move the shape through the key for quartal comping on the static spots (Softly's A, Moanin'). For the boogie-ish list, a minor pentatonic in the low octave (C E♭ F G B♭) is already a boogie bass line.

Form and bandstand words

Head
The melody chorus, played first and last.
Chorus
One complete pass through the form (32 bars here). "Take two" means two choruses.
Turnaround
The last two bars' I–VI–II–V (or a variant) that leads back to the top.
Tag
Repeating the last phrase, usually three times, to end.
Vamp
A repeated two- or four-bar figure: an intro, or "till ready" for the singer.
Break
The band stops for two bars and the soloist plays alone.
Trading fours
Soloists alternate four-bar phrases, often with the drummer. Eights likewise.
In two / in four
Bass in half notes / walking quarters. Head in two, solos in four is the Basie shape.
Shout chorus
The loud arranged ensemble chorus near the end.
Out chorus
The last melody chorus; "take it out" means go to the ending.
Rhythm changes
The chords of I Got Rhythm. "Rhythm bridge": III7–VI7–II7–V7.
Honeysuckle bridge
I7–IV–II7–V7 (also called the Montgomery-Ward bridge).
Contrafact
A new melody on an existing tune's changes (Oleo on rhythm changes, Scrapple on Honeysuckle).
Roman numerals
Chords named by scale degree: in B♭, I = B♭, ii = C–7, V7 = F7. Lower case = minor; "7" = dominant.
ii–V–I
The basic cadence: D–7 G7 C. Minor version: Dø7 G7♭9 C–.
Secondary dominant
A dominant pointing at a chord other than I: D7 → G7 in C (V of V).
Tritone sub
A dominant replaced by the dominant a tritone away: D♭7 for G7. Same 3rd and 7th, swapped.
Line cliché
A moving inner voice under a held chord: C–, C–Δ7, C–7, C–6 (Blue Skies, It Don't Mean a Thing).
Pedal
A held bass note under changing chords (the B♭ under the Blue Skies bridge).

Solo-map words

Guide tones
The 3rd and 7th of each chord, and the line they trace through the changes.
Money note
The one note that names a chord in its context (G♯ on A Train's D7♭5). Highlighted in the solo maps.
Mixolydian
Major scale with ♭7; the dominant scale. G7 → G A B C D E F.
Dorian
Minor scale with a natural 6; the minor-7 scale. D–7 → D E F G A B C.
Lydian dominant
Mixolydian with ♯4. D7♯11 → D E F♯ G♯ A B C.
Harmonic minor
Minor with a raised 7; use over V7♭9 in minor (C harmonic minor over G7♭9).
Melodic minor
Minor with raised 6 and 7; the –6 and –Δ7 scale.
Altered scale
For 7alt chords: melodic minor a half step up (D♭ melodic minor over C7alt).
Diminished scale
Alternating half and whole steps; for °7 chords and 7♭9 chords.
Pentatonic
Five notes: major 1 2 3 5 6, minor 1 ♭3 4 5 ♭7. See the pentatonics page.
Blues scale
Minor pentatonic plus the ♭5.
Enclosure
Approaching a target note from above and below before landing on it.
Rootless voicing
A left-hand chord without the root (3‑5‑7‑9 or 7‑9‑3‑5); the bass has the root.
Shell
Two notes: 3rd and 7th (or root and 7th). Enough at fast tempos.
Block chords
Melody harmonised with four notes in the right hand, doubled an octave down in the left (Shearing, Garner, Timmons). Also "locked hands".
Stride
Left hand alternating a bass note (beats 1 and 3) with a chord (2 and 4). Waller, Johnson.
Comping
Accompanying a soloist with chords and rhythm (from "accompanying").
Charleston rhythm
A dotted quarter and an eighth: hits on beat 1 and the "and" of 2. The standard comping figure.
Quartal voicing
A chord built in fourths (D G C E A, the "So What" voicing).

Tuxedo Junction · your key B♭

Erskine Hawkins, Bill Johnson & Julian Dash, 1939 (Glenn Miller's 1940 hit). Confirmed AABA (Ethan Iverson lists it among the swing-era AABA riff tunes), and the vocal chord sheets give the A strain (I7–IV7 riff, with a ♯IV°–I–VI7–ii–V turnaround), but none of them shows the instrumental bridge. Your chart would settle it in five minutes.

Cute

Neal Hefti for Basie, 1958. Turns out to be an arrangement rather than a jam tune: an eight-bar piano intro, a call-and-response head with brushes, then blues changes under the solos and a 32-bar drum feature (per the Freddie Green and Lush Life transcriptions). Replaced in the book by Perdido.

The Grabtown Grapple

Artie Shaw & Buster Harding; Shaw's Gramercy Five, Victor, 9 January 1945, with Roy Eldridge, Dodo Marmarosa and Barney Kessel. A head arrangement built on riffs, with an eight-bar bridge, so by the sound of it a 32-bar A A B A. Not on Wikipedia's rhythm-changes list, so the A is probably not plain rhythm changes. Miser's Serenade

Full title "Gimme, Gimme, Gimme, Gimme (The Miser's Serenade)", by Fred Patrick, Claude Reese, Marvin Fisher and Jack Val. Chris Connor with the Sy Oliver Orchestra, December 1953: Bethlehem's first single. A novelty vocal; no lead sheet was ever published.

Lifting a riff tune off a record

  1. Find the key: the last bass note of the record is the tonic nine times out of ten. Check it against the piano.
  2. Count the form in 8s. Where the riff changes is the bridge. If the riff never changes and the tune is 12 bars, it's a blues: stop here, you know it.
  3. The bridge test (bar 17): does the bass go to III7 (rhythm bridge: D7 in B♭), to I7 heading for IV (Honeysuckle bridge: B♭7 → E♭), or straight to IV (A-Train bridge: E♭)? Those three cover almost every swing-era riff tune.
  4. The A test (bar 5): does it push to IV (I7–IV–♯IV°–I, the rhythm/riff A) or stay home on I with a I–VI–II–V turnaround?
  5. Turnarounds are I–VI–II–V unless you hear something odd. Write it in, play it with the record, fix the one bar that clashes.
